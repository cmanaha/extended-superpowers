Motivation & data

It started with telemetry.

This has been a journey of measuring my own work. I instrument my projects, so I read that telemetry and Claude Code's /insights to see where my time actually went, and I ran a few small experiments to check it. Over about four months they pointed the same way: I was getting more leverage from how I work. What follows is what the data showed.

I measure things, in the systems I build and in how I work. Reading my own session data wasn't about chasing a number. I wanted to see plainly whether the way I work was actually buying me leverage, and where it wasn't. The charts below are drawn from my own git history, my session transcripts, and my /insights reports. Every one carries its source and its caveats.

A note on scope: these are personal projects I build at home, on my own time. None of this is my work, and the data on this page does not include or reflect anything I do professionally.

1 · This has been my default for about four months.

It's a habit by now. The same scaffold (CI gates, decision records, specs, plans, agent review) shows up from my earliest 2026 repo, and became automatic in every repo I started after.

2 · I spend my time deciding what to build, then let the agents run.

The data reads like dictation, not back-and-forth. The gaps between my messages are long because the agents are working in them, and in a long session they take many steps of work for each thing I say.

3 · Some of the work runs for hours, on its own.

Most sessions are short. But a real share of them run for hours of continuous build-and-verify while I'm doing something else. The longest I measured was around nine hours unbroken.

4 · Often, more than one is running at once.

Usually that means two repositories at once. While one project is grinding through implementation, with its subagents and research running, I start the next piece on another project. The sessions aren't the same length: a spec or a research pass is often minutes to an hour, while an implementation run is the long one, sometimes around four hours. I let the long one run and work the short ones in the meantime.

5 · Directionally, more of it lands.

Across two windows a week apart, the share of sessions that mostly or fully achieved what I set out to do moved up. One small movement, not a trend I'd stake anything on, but it points the right way.

6 · My friction is choosing what to build, not fixing what got built.

This is the one that changed how I work. When I read where my sessions actually go wrong, the top two causes weren't bugs. They were "wrong approach" and "misunderstood request", both about scoping. The rigor layer (tests, review, the done-gate) takes correctness off my plate; what's left is deciding the right thing to build. That is why the loop front-loads research and review, and why the research phase exists at all.

What this is, and what it isn't

  • This is personal: home projects on my own time. None of it is my work, and the numbers here don't include anything I do professionally.
  • It's a single person's data, over about four months. There's no control group and no baseline to compare against.
  • The satisfaction, outcome, and friction labels come from Claude reading my sessions; they're model-inferred, not measured ground truth.
  • I can't perfectly separate my interactive work from automated and evaluation runs, so I've kept the durations concrete and treated the ratios as indicative.
  • I've deliberately left out the bigger "multiplier" numbers I sometimes see in my own telemetry. They're my own rough estimates, and this page is for the things I can actually point to.

None of this proves the loop is better than some other way of working. It's just what I found when I looked: long autonomous stretches, thin steering, and my own time going into what to build rather than into debugging. That's the shape that made me write the loop down, and share it.

Most of this (the plugin, this site, the code behind it) wasn't written at a keyboard. It was dictated to a pair of smart glasses, hands-free, in between other things. I rarely used the console.
